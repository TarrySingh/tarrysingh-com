I have sat in enough boardrooms over thirty years to know the difference between a skills shortage and a skills story. The shortage is real — I will get to the numbers. But the story Europe tells itself about fixing it through grand infrastructure announcements is, at best, half the job. We are building compute. We are still under-building people.

Let me lay out the actual state of AI education in Europe as it stands in mid-2026, demand side first, because that is where the pain is measured.

## what employers are actually short of

Start with the headline that should worry every board.

94% of leaders face AI-critical skill shortages today, with one in three reporting gaps of 40% or more.

That is from the World Economic Forum's October 2025 work, and it tracks with what the WEF's own Future of Jobs survey found:

skill gaps are categorically considered the biggest barrier to business transformation, with 63% of employers identifying them as a major barrier over the 2025–2030 period.

Now read that alongside the discipline gap.

Only 46% of organizations currently integrate workforce planning into their AI roadmaps.

McKinsey's Next Era of Work survey is blunter on the European side:

while 73 percent of HR leaders claim to plan strategically, only 12% of European organizations are looking ahead three years or beyond.

And

31% of European leaders cite limited visibility into current skills as an issue.

So the pain point is not just "we can't hire AI engineers." It is that most firms cannot see what they have, cannot say what they will need, and are recruiting reactively into the most competitive labour market in a generation.

Cities such as London, Paris, Berlin, Amsterdam and Madrid are facing some of the most significant talent shortages anywhere in the world.

The profile of who they want has also shifted under their feet.

Companies are no longer looking only for deep technical experts. They are searching for hybrid talent: people who understand AI technologies but can also connect them to business strategy, communicate with non-technical teams and think creatively about real-world applications.

The pure model-builder is still scarce and expensive. The person who can build, govern, and translate is rarer still — and that is the person universities are least set up to produce.

## what Europe's universities are producing

Here is the genuinely good news, and I do not get to say this often about European competitiveness. The continent is an education powerhouse on paper.

35 percent of all AI-related Master programmes globally are offered by EU universities and research centres, with Germany, France and the Netherlands at the forefront.

AI talent itself

has more than doubled in the EU between 2016 and 2023, now representing 0.41% of the EU workforce.

The programme catalogue is deep and increasingly cross-border. Erasmus Mundus joint masters now stitch together four or five universities per degree —

EMAI, for instance, runs across Pompeu Fabra in Barcelona, Sapienza in Rome, Radboud in Nijmegen and Ljubljana, integrating machine learning with modern symbolic AI across theory, methods, and applications.

The Connecting Europe Facility funded four targeted masters years ago,

designed by consortia of universities, SMEs and research centres focusing on human-centric AI, AI ethics, AI for the public sector and AI in healthcare.

I have skin in this. I am a founding contributor to the Human Centered AI Masters —

developed by Technological University Dublin, HU Utrecht, Naples Federico II and Budapest, training specialists with knowledge of AI ethics and regulation alongside competences to apply it in real-world situations.

The pedagogy is sound. The problem is throughput and timing, not intent.

Two leaks in that diagram are worth dwelling on. The first is gender.

Women represent less than one-quarter of AI engineers across Europe, and as little as 11% in some cities.

You cannot solve a structural talent shortage while ignoring half the population at the top of the funnel. The second is geography.

Europe's AI workforce is unevenly internationalised — while the EU attracts many foreign-trained professionals, most talent inflows concentrate in a handful of countries, notably Germany.

A Portuguese or Greek graduate trained at EU expense too often ends up in Munich, London, or San Francisco.

## the policy layer — ambitious, late, compute-heavy

Brussels has not been idle, and I want to be fair about that. On

9 April 2025 the Commission published the AI Continent Action Plan, aiming to increase the offer of European bachelor's, master's and PhD degrees in AI and to launch the AI Skills Academy.

The Academy is meant to

develop a pilot generative-AI degree and run scholarship and returnship schemes to attract more women to the field.

The

Apply AI Strategy followed in October 2025 to support adoption across strategic sectors.

And since

Article 4 of the AI Act entered into application on 2 February 2025, providers and deployers must ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among staff.

That literacy mandate is the most underrated lever in the whole package. It quietly turns every regulated employer into a training provider.

Now the money.

At the Paris AI Action Summit, von der Leyen launched InvestAI to mobilise €200 billion for investment in AI, including a new €20 billion European fund for AI gigafactories.

National plans stack on top:

France announced more than €100bn in AI investment commitments in February 2025, Germany has permanently funded national AI Competence Centres, and Portugal's agenda implies up to 1.3 million workers may need retraining by 2030.

Here is where I get uncomfortable. Compare the orders of magnitude. The dedicated GenAI skills academy call carried a budget of

€7 million to help build a digital skills academy in GenAI

— against €20 billion for gigafactories. Two thousand to one, compute over curriculum. I understand the logic of sovereign infrastructure. But Europe already

does not manufacture the class of cutting-edge AI chips needed to populate those gigafactories, and most of the roughly 100,000 accelerators per site will be imported.

We are borrowing silicon to look sovereign while starving the one input we genuinely own — educated people.

## the mismatch nobody costs properly

The OECD has put its finger on the part the headline numbers miss. After surveying government training programmes across member countries, it concluded that

current training supply may not be sufficient to meet the growing need for general AI literacy skills.

Specialised masters are not the constraint — Europe has 800-plus of them. The constraint is the long tail: the accountant, the nurse, the logistics planner who now has an AI tool on their desk and no framework for judging its output.

This matters more in Europe than the US precisely because of a cultural lag I see in every engagement.

European leaders and employees are roughly ten percentage points less likely than their North American counterparts to expect their current role will meaningfully change in the next one to two years.

That complacency is the real risk. The technology will change the role whether the role-holder expects it or not.

And the macro stakes are not subtle. The WEF projects that

while overall job numbers grow by 170 million globally by 2030, roughly 90 million workers will be displaced based on the gap between existing and emerging skills.

Europe's demographic position — ageing, shrinking working-age population — means it cannot afford to waste a single trainable worker.

## my calibrated take

If I were on a European board, or advising a minister, I would push capital the other way: heavier into curriculum, apprenticeships, and mid-career conversion, lighter on the trophy gigafactories whose chips we import anyway. The talent equation, as the interface researchers framed it, is whether Europe will

nurture local AI talent, attract specialists from abroad, or watch its expertise emigrate to competing innovation hubs.

Right now we are doing all three at once, and the third is winning on the margins.

The universities are not the weak link. The catalogue is world-class —

Europe has committed roughly €20 billion to AI while projecting around 21% growth in ICT professions.

The weak link is the join: between graduate and employer, between literacy mandate and actual training budget, between announcement and intake.

I would bet against any European AI strategy that spends three orders of magnitude more on machines than on the people who must run them. Fix the join. The talent is already here — we keep letting it walk out the door.
